Sister Ellen Savage and the hospital ship Centaur, 1943

What can the records prove about who sank a marked hospital ship, and whether it was legitimately protected?

The exhibits on The Page — a reconstructed casualty telegram, the 1943 photograph, a reconstructed news bulletin, and a typeset extract from the Newcastle Morning Herald — belong with this file. Read them before you write a finding.

What is agreed

On 14 May 1943, shortly after 4 a.m., the 2/3rd Australian Hospital Ship Centaur was sunk off the Queensland coast. She had left Sydney unescorted on 12 May, bound north, carrying her crew and medical staff, including members of the 2/12th Field Ambulance, and no patients. The Australian War Memorial places the sinking south of Moreton Island; an older Memorial encyclopedia entry gave a position of about 27°17′ S, 153°58′ E, about 50 miles east-north-east of Brisbane. Some later accounts give the time as 4.10 a.m. This file uses the Memorial’s wording, “shortly after 4 am,” and notes the variation.

A contemporary correction, reported on 20 May 1943, said that a further check by Australian Army and Navy authorities had revised an earlier figure of 363 people on board down to 332, “with a corresponding diminution in casualties.” The Australian War Memorial uses the figures 332 on board and 64 survivors (268 people died). Some later summaries give 322 on board. This file uses the Memorial’s 332 and 64, and records that later figures sometimes differ. Twelve nursing sisters were on board. Only Sister Ellen Savage, Australian Army Nursing Service, survived.

Period photographs, including Australian War Memorial 302796, a port-side view taken in Sydney in 1943 and now in the public domain, show a white hull, a green band, and prominent red crosses on the hull, the funnel, and the stern, with another cross lying horizontally on the after deckhouse. The Memorial has described the ship as appropriately lit and marked as a hospital ship. One Memorial exhibition page calls her “brightly-lit” at the time she was hit. A photograph of the ship in harbour, and a later official wording about lights, do not by themselves prove what a submarine commander could see at night.

The sinking was very rapid. One Memorial article says the ship sank in about three minutes. Some survivors, quoted in a 20 May 1943 newspaper report, said ballast in her bottom helped to send her under “in a few seconds.” Seaman Martin Pash, as quoted by the Australian War Memorial, said the Centaur “just went straight down.” The 64 survivors spent about a day and a half in the water and on rafts before they were spotted by a RAAF aircraft and taken off by the United States destroyer USS Mugford, as recorded by the Memorial.

Sister Ellen Savage, service number NFX.76584, was the only nurse to survive. The London Gazette of 25 August 1944 recorded the award of the George Medal to “Sister (Miss) Ellen Savage, NFX.76584, Australian Army Nursing Service,” “in recognition of conspicuous gallantry in carrying out hazardous work in a very brave manner.” Two days after the rescue she was interviewed in hospital by Mervyn Weston; that interview was printed in the Newcastle Morning Herald and Miners’ Advocate, 19 May 1943, p. 2. The paper called her Eleanor; other reports used Nellie. The Gazette names her Ellen Savage.

The Australian Government sent an official protest to Japan through Switzerland. The Advocate (Burnie), 20 May 1943, reported that Prime Minister John Curtin “stated emphatically” that all those on board were non-combatants, “members of a medical corps,” and that the Army legal branch held the protection of Army medical personnel on a hospital ship was “clearly expressed in the Geneva and Hague conventions,” and was not lost because there were no sick or wounded on board. The Australian War Memorial later summarised that Japan “did not acknowledge responsibility for the incident for many years,” and that “the War Crimes Tribunal could not identify the responsible submarine.” A later Japanese official war history, as summarised by the Memorial, attributed the sinking to submarine I-177 under Lieutenant Commander Nakagawa. This file records both summaries, and does not treat the later official history as a courtroom finding.

Green on the printed page marks what can be agreed. Amber, used in Super Sleuths, marks what is still disputed or was added later.

What they said. Savage said her best friend, Sister King, was in the next cabin, and that she heard her shout, “Quick, Savage, up on deck.” They had often talked about what they would do if they were ever torpedoed. Sister King could not swim, “and it had been agreed that I should crocodile her — that is, take her across my shoulders — if we ever finished in the water.” “So, after meeting on deck, we jumped into the water together. Sister King was apparently killed by debris, for I never saw her again. It was then that I apparently injured my side, but I did not realise it at the time. I am a strong swimmer, and immediately struck away from the ship when I realised that Sister King had disappeared.” She said she climbed onto a section of the deck-house with Robert Westwood, “the 15-year-old cabin boy, who had burns,” and that they worked their way to two rafts “on which nearly 20 men were huddling.” A man gave her a greatcoat. “I gave Westwood what attention I could, and sheltered him against me with the greatcoat. This great coat undoubtedly saved my life.”

Could they really have seen it? She was on the ship when it was hit, and in the water afterwards. She could speak about what she heard, what she did, and whom she could no longer find. She could not see who fired the torpedo, or from which submarine, or what that person could see of the markings and the lights. She was injured, newly rescued, and speaking to a reporter. A newspaper interview is still a reporter’s wording of what she said. Accounts of her injuries are not identical across the 19 May article and later Memorial quotations.

Why might they say it? She was asked, two days after the rescue, to say what had happened. She named her friend, the plan they had made, a boy she tried to help, and a coat that kept her alive. That is evidence of the sinking as a person lived it. It is not evidence of who fired, or of whether the ship’s markings could be seen from a submarine at night.

The hospital ship Centaur, as marked

2/3rd Australian Hospital Ship · Photographed in Sydney, 1943 · Officially described as marked and lit

The ship herself, speaking through a 1943 photograph and through official descriptions of her markings and lights.

Source. Australian War Memorial 302796, “Port side view of the hospital ship Centaur,” Sydney, 1943. The Memorial’s caption notes the red crosses and green lines on the hull, funnel, stern, and after deckhouse. Copyright expired — public domain. Official wording on lighting and marking is from the Australian War Memorial, including an exhibition page that calls the ship “brightly-lit” when she was hit. The Advocate (Burnie), 20 May 1943, records the Army legal branch on the Geneva and Hague conventions.

What they said. The photograph shows a white hospital ship with a dark band along the hull and large crosses painted on her side. The Memorial’s caption names the band as green, and the crosses as red, and points to further crosses on the funnel, the stern, and the after deckhouse. The Memorial has said the ship “had been appropriately lit and marked to indicate that it was a hospital ship,” and that her sinking “was regarded as an atrocity.” In May 1943 the Army legal branch, as reported at the time, said the protection of Army medical personnel on a hospital ship was clearly expressed in the Geneva and Hague conventions, and was not lost because there were no sick or wounded on board.

Could they really have seen it? The camera, in Sydney harbour in 1943, could record how the ship was painted. It could not record how those markings looked from a submarine at sea, shortly after four in the morning, off the Queensland coast. An official description that the ship was lit and marked is strong evidence of how the Allies presented her. It is not, by itself, proof of what the person who fired could see, or of whether a notification of her identity — if one was sent, and if it arrived — had been read. Some later official and education accounts say her identification was passed to the International Committee of the Red Cross and to Japan in early February 1943. This file has not inspected the original notification papers, and does not treat that date as proved here.

Why might they say it? Hospital ships were painted and lit so that, under the conventions, they could be recognised and left alone. After the sinking, official Australian voices had a reason to stress those markings, the lights, and the legal protection. That does not make the photograph untrue. It does mean students should ask what the markings can prove, and what they cannot.

The official Japanese record, and Lieutenant Commander Hajime Nakagawa

A 1943 denial, a post-war investigation that did not identify the submarine, and a later official war history · Nakagawa, named in that later history, never commented on the Centaur

What was officially said, and not said, about who fired. This card is a record, not a confession.

Source. The Australian War Memorial encyclopedia entry “Centaur (Hospital ship),” as archived, summarises that Japan did not acknowledge responsibility for many years, that “the War Crimes Tribunal could not identify the responsible submarine,” and that a later Japanese official war history attributed the sinking to submarine I-177 under Lieutenant Commander Nakagawa. The same entry records that Nakagawa was convicted for firing on survivors of the British ship Chivalry — a different case, not a trial for the Centaur. This file has not inspected the war history or the investigation papers, and does not invent words for Nakagawa. He did not comment. The image is a silhouette, not a likeness.

What they said. In 1943, as the Memorial later put it, Japan did not accept responsibility. The Australian Government had already sent a protest through Switzerland. After the war, investigators, as summarised by the Memorial, could not identify the responsible submarine for a war-crimes prosecution. A later Japanese official war history, again as summarised by the Memorial, “makes clear that it was submarine I-177, under the command of Lt Commander Nakagawa who had sunk the Centaur.” Nakagawa himself left no statement on the Centaur for this file to quote. Some later naval histories have pointed to other Japanese submarines then off the Australian east coast, and have not all agreed on I-177.

Could they really have seen it? A government can deny, or later record, an attack it did not publicly accept at the time. A war-crimes investigation can fail to name a boat to a criminal standard, and still leave a suspicion on the file. A later official history can name a submarine and a commander without that naming having been tested in court. None of those records can show us the view from a periscope at night. Nakagawa could not be cross-examined, in this file, about what he saw, or whether he saw a hospital ship at all. He made no comment.

Why might they say it? In 1943 a denial would have rejected an official Australian charge of an atrocity. A later official history, written long afterwards, may have been a historical accounting, not a confession in court. The honest position, from this file, is that the records name I-177 in a later official history, and do not name it in a completed prosecution.

Why it still matters

Hospital ships, and the rules meant to protect them, are still argued over when a marked ship is hit. The Centaur is commemorated in Australia as an atrocity; the question of who fired, and of what could be seen, has never been settled in a court. Families of the dead, and the wreck itself — found in 2009 and protected as a historic shipwreck and a memorial — are not a puzzle for students to solve. A careful finding will say what the 1943 records and the later official summaries can show, and what they cannot.

Super Sleuths

Go further only if you want to. Amber marks what is still disputed, or what later writers added.

1. What could be seen at night?

Allied official sources, including the Australian War Memorial, describe the Centaur as clearly marked and brightly lit. The 1943 photograph in this file shows the markings in harbour, in daylight. A submarine at periscope depth, shortly after 4 a.m., is a different point of view. Some later official and education accounts say her identification was passed to the International Committee of the Red Cross and to Japan in early February 1943. This file has not inspected those notification papers. A notification, even if it was sent and received, is not the same as a commander seeing a red cross in the dark.

Question. What can a photograph of a marked ship, an official wording about lights, and a claimed notification, each prove — and what can none of them prove about the view from a submarine?

2. Who fired? The closed file and the later history

The Memorial’s encyclopedia entry records that post-war investigators could not identify the responsible submarine, and that a later Japanese official war history attributed the sinking to I-177 under Lieutenant Commander Hajime Nakagawa. Nakagawa did not comment on the Centaur. He was convicted of other war crimes, for firing on survivors of another ship, not for this one. Some later naval histories have pointed to other Japanese submarines then off the east coast, and have not all agreed on I-177. This file does not treat the 1979 history, as reported by the Memorial, as a courtroom finding, and it does not invent a confession.

Question. If a later official history names a submarine, and a completed prosecution never did, what should a careful finding say?

3. Cargo, rifles, and the conventions

After the sinking, and in later education materials, claims have been made about weapons and cargo: that ambulance drivers tried to bring rifles and ammunition aboard; that the Master and the Chief Medical Officer questioned what the conventions allowed; that a search was made; that figures of about 52 rifles and 2,000 or 4,000 rounds appear in later retellings. Those figures do not all match, even in the same later materials. The Hague conventions allowed some arms for the maintenance of order and the defence of the wounded. The Memorial’s main accounts of the sinking emphasise the markings, the lights, and the loss of life, not a cargo of munitions. This file does not settle how many rifles, if any, were in a hold, and it does not use an unsettled cargo claim to decide whether the ship was legitimately protected.

Question. Why might later writers argue about cargo and rifles, and why should a student keep those claims in amber until a named primary source is on the desk?

4. Numbers that moved

In the week after the sinking, a reported official correction revised 363 people on board down to 332. The Memorial uses 332 and 64 survivors. Some later pages say 322. Figures for the 2/12th Field Ambulance also vary between later captions and later articles (for example, 14 survivors from “two hundred,” or 178 dead from 193). This file uses the Memorial’s 332 and 64, and the correction reported on 20 May 1943, and leaves the smaller disagreements in amber.

Question. Why might official numbers change in the first week, and why does a later disagreement about a unit total not undo the fact of the sinking?

5. The wreck is a memorial, not a hunt

The wreck was found on 20 December 2009, about 30 nautical miles off the southern tip of Moreton Island, in deep water, and is now a protected historic shipwreck and a memorial. That discovery is part of the later history of the case. It is not the student question, and the site is not to be treated as a puzzle or a treasure hunt.

Question. Why might finding the wreck still leave the question of who fired, and of what could be seen, unsettled?

The narrator is an invented present-day journalist, not a real person, and is not based on a named reporter. The journalist is shown looking through four artefacts before deciding to reopen the case. Sensitivities: war dead, families, and injuries

Two hundred and sixty-eight people died. Teach the sinking with care. Do not use photographs of human remains, or of bodies in the water, if they exist. Do not invent last words, panic, or suffering beyond what a named source actually says. Accounts of Savage’s injuries are not identical; do not tidy them into one medical list. A 15-year-old cabin boy with burns is in the 1943 interview. Handle that line carefully in a Year 10 classroom. The case is not a First Nations history as a whole, but a contemporary crew-survivor list printed on 20 May 1943 named E. M. Salt as a “Torres strait pilot.” This file does not examine that person’s story. If you take it further, do so with care, and with consultation if a Torres Strait Islander’s experience is taught in any depth.

I-177 and Nakagawa: do not settle a closed prosecution

Present the conflict, do not close it. The Memorial records that the war-crimes process could not identify the submarine, and that a later Japanese official war history named I-177 and Lieutenant Commander Hajime Nakagawa. Nakagawa did not comment on the Centaur. He died in 1991, so he is not a living person, but legal care still applies to how he is named: he was convicted of other crimes, not tried for this one. Do not print a student-facing finding that treats him as the settled offender. Some later naval histories have preferred other boats then off the east coast. If you use later press reports of a Japanese embassy comment after the 2009 wreck discovery, quote a named article, and do not treat a diplomatic wording as a verdict either way.

Markings, lights, notification, and cargo

Allied official sources say the ship was marked and lit. That is evidence. Students should still ask what a submarine commander could see, and what a claimed notification to Japan can actually prove. This file has not inspected original ICRC or Japanese notification papers, and so does not treat a February 1943 notification date as agreed fact. Cargo and rifle claims belong in Super Sleuths. Figures in later education materials conflict. Do not use an unsettled munitions story to decide the legal protection of a hospital ship, and do not invent a hidden cargo.

Names, numbers, and sources not used

1943 newspapers used Eleanor and Nellie as well as Ellen; the Gazette and the Memorial use Ellen Savage, NFX.76584. An early official total of 363 was corrected to 332 in May 1943. Prefer the Memorial’s 332 and 64, and flag later 322s. The AWM newsreel F00734, in which Savage was interviewed from a hospital bed, is not treated as public domain and is not used as a still in this file. A longer wording sometimes circulated as her George Medal “citation” has not been verified from the Gazette page inspected for this file; only the Gazette’s published award wording is used. A recommendation is held by the Memorial (RC02373) but is not quoted here.
